Why Your Browser Shows a Warning

The first time you connect over HTTPS, your browser will say the connection is not private. Chrome shows "Your connection is not private", Firefox shows "Warning: Potential Security Risk Ahead" and Safari shows "This Connection Is Not Private".

This is expected and it does not mean something is broken. HTTPS certificates are normally issued by a certificate authority that verifies you own a public domain name. Your MyNode runs on your own network and has no public domain name, so it acts as its own certificate authority instead. Your browser does not know that authority until you install it, so it warns you rather than showing a padlock.

The connection is still encrypted. The warning is about identity, not about encryption.

Continuing to Your Node

You need to accept the certificate once per browser and device, and again whenever your node renews it, which happens about every two years and whenever its IP address changes. {% if ca_info %}Trusting your node, below, stops the warning for good.{% endif %}

Chrome, Edge and Brave — click "Advanced", then "Proceed to ... (unsafe)".

Firefox — click "Advanced", then "Accept the Risk and Continue".

Safari — click "Show Details", then "visit this website", and confirm.

iOS and Android — tap the advanced or details option on the warning page, then continue to the site.

If the warning has no option to continue, your browser remembers trusting an earlier certificate for that address and now refuses to accept any other. {% if ca_info %}Install the certificate authority below, or reach{% else %}Reach{% endif %} your node by its IP address instead, which is never blocked this way.

Trusting Your Node

Your MyNode is its own certificate authority. Install it on a computer or phone and that device trusts your node's HTTPS pages from then on, with a padlock and no warning. You only do this once per device: your node renews its certificate automatically, and every renewal is trusted without reinstalling anything.

1. Download it. You may be asked to log in first. It is also on the settings page under HTTPS Certificates.

Download Certificate Authority

2. Check it is yours. Once installed, your system shows the certificate's name and SHA-256 fingerprint. They should match these. If you have more than one MyNode, the name tells their authorities apart.
Name: {{ ca_info.name }}
SHA-256: {{ ca_info.fingerprint }}

3. Install it.

macOS — open the downloaded file to add it to Keychain Access, then double-click "{{ ca_info.name }}", open Trust and set "When using this certificate" to "Always Trust".

Windows — open the downloaded file, click "Install Certificate", choose "Current User", then place it in "Trusted Root Certification Authorities".

Linux — copy the file into the system's certificate folder and refresh it. On Debian and Ubuntu: sudo cp mynode_ca.crt /usr/local/share/ca-certificates/ && sudo update-ca-certificates On Fedora: sudo cp mynode_ca.crt /etc/pki/ca-trust/source/anchors/ && sudo update-ca-trust Browsers on Linux may keep their own list instead. If Chrome still warns, open Settings, then Privacy and security, Security, Manage certificates, and import it as a trusted authority. For Firefox, see below.

Firefox (on any system) — open Settings, then Privacy & Security, View Certificates, Authorities, Import, and tick "Trust this CA to identify websites".

iOS — open the file in Safari and allow the profile download, install it from Settings under "Profile Downloaded", then turn it on under Settings, General, About, Certificate Trust Settings. The certificate is not trusted until that last step.

Android — open Settings, then Security, Encryption & credentials, Install a certificate, CA certificate. The exact menu names vary by phone.

Your certificate authority is generated on your node and is unique to it. It can only vouch for local network names and addresses, so it cannot be used to impersonate other websites. Only install one you downloaded from your own MyNode.

The certificate authority is kept on your node's drive, so reflashing the OS drive keeps it. Reformatting the drive or setting up a new node creates a new one: remove the old one from your devices and install the new one. Until you do, your browser warns again, and for up to an hour it may refuse to open your node's name at all. Its IP address always works.
